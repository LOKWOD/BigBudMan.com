Henola:
the agronomic field note.

Agronomic, consumer-literacy, and recordkeeping education for adults 21+—not legal advice, seed-sale authorization, a cultivation plan, a crop recommendation, a variety-authentication service, or a yield, cannabinoid, food, feed, or processing guarantee. Hemp production, seed certification, intellectual-property rights, testing, transport, processing, and finished-product rules vary. Verify the current rule and qualified local guidance before acquiring, producing, moving, or processing seed or crop material.

THE USEFUL PART

Keep these in your head.

  • USDA's Plant Variety Protection certificate identifies Henola as hemp variety PV 202100192 and grants protection dated December 10, 2021. The certificate is an identity and intellectual-property record—not a retail seed endorsement or crop-performance warranty.
  • USDA ARS GRIN records PI 699397 as Cannabis sativa L. cultivar Henola, received as seed by the Plant Variety Protection Voucher Collection in 2021, and says that accession is not available. A GRIN record is not a shopping link.
  • Current university trial material places Henola in the grain and oilseed lane. That purpose does not establish a CBD-flower profile, aroma, effect, fixed cannabinoid result, or lawful status for every seed lot and harvest.
  • Trial results belong to the named location, season, design, seed source, management, and measured outcome. Do not copy a yield, flowering date, plant height, or chemistry result into a promise for another farm or package.
  • Authenticate the exact seed and lot through the responsible seller, invoice, variety name, lot identifier, certification or intellectual-property documents when applicable, and the production program that governs the location.

Preserve disagreement instead of smoothing it over

The reviewed USDA materials do not tell one perfectly tidy origin story. The GRIN accession page displays “Developed – Germany,” names International Hemp, LLC as a developer, and connects the accession to PV 202100192. The USDA PVP certificate names the Institute of Natural Fibers and Medicinal Plant as the certificate holder. UC Davis describes Henola as a recent variety from Poland.

Those fields may reflect different concepts—development record, applicant or rights holder, seed source, or national breeding context—but this page does not invent the missing bridge. Preserve the exact source, field label, date, and document number. When provenance matters for a transaction, certification, license, or rights question, ask the responsible office or rights holder rather than collapsing “Germany,” “Poland,” “developer,” and “applicant” into one unsupported sentence.

Use the 2025 trial as a worked example—not a forecast

UC Davis reports that its 2025 Northern California trial used a randomized complete block design with four replications and evaluated eight commercial hemp cultivars. Its table labels Henola “Grain, oil seed” and notes early flowering in that site-year group. The study also describes plot size, seeding density, harvest sampling, drying, cleaning, and separate compliance sampling.

That transparency makes the report useful because readers can see what was compared. It also exposes the boundary: Davis, California in 2025 is not every latitude, soil, water regime, disease pressure, license, harvest system, or market. The report itself says multi-location and genotype-by-environment analysis matter. A trial ranking is not a bag label, and a preliminary report is not a guarantee.

  • Record site, year, experimental design, replications, entry source, planting rate, harvest method, and measured trait.
  • Compare only the same outcome and unit; grain yield, stem biomass, bast proportion, maturity, oil quality, and compliance are different questions.
  • Do not transfer a result across regions without local replicated evidence and qualified agronomic interpretation.
  • Keep “statistically different,” “numerically higher,” and “commercially useful” separate.

Pass the production and product gates separately

  1. Production authorization.

    USDA states that hemp producers must be licensed or authorized under the applicable state, Tribal, or USDA program. A cultivar name does not replace that authorization.

  2. Seed and rights.

    Check the current PVP status, seller authority, certification documents, import or movement rules, and any contract restrictions for the exact seed and jurisdiction.

  3. Sampling and testing.

    Follow the governing program's current sampling, laboratory, reporting, remediation, and disposal rules. Do not copy a historic trial result onto a new field.

  4. Finished-product authority.

    Food, feed, supplement, cosmetic, seed, oil, meal, fiber, and other products can fall under different federal, state, and local authorities. Production compliance does not automatically authorize every end use.

  5. Transport and sale.

    Preserve licenses, certificates, shipping records, lot identity, and destination requirements. Do not assume a hemp word on an invoice answers every state-line or product question.
