Industrial oilseed hemp cultivar field note

Finola is a variety record.
Not a product promise.

Use the official name and oilseed description as the start of an evidence chain—not as proof of a seller's seed lot, lawful production, crop compliance, CBD content, field performance, finished-product identity, aroma, effect, or medical value.

Hemp licensing, approved cultivars, seed movement, sampling, total-THC testing, harvest, processing, food and finished-product rules vary. Check the current official program and qualified local guidance before acquiring, producing, moving, processing, or selling hemp material.

THE USEFUL PART

Keep these in your head.

  • The Canadian Food Inspection Agency describes Finola—previous denomination FIN-314—as an early-maturing, dioecious oilseed hemp cultivar and records the named breeders. That official identity record does not authenticate a retail seed lot or publish a consumer effect profile.
  • Health Canada's 2026 List of Approved Cultivars includes Finola and Finola2 for licensed Canadian industrial-hemp production. The list is dated and jurisdiction-specific; it is not a worldwide approval or finished-product authorization.
  • U.S. hemp compliance follows the applicable state, Tribal, or USDA production program. USDA requires representative lot sampling and total-THC testing; a cultivar name cannot substitute for the crop lot's official compliance process.
  • Oilseed purpose is not a CBD-content claim. Hemp seed oil and cannabinoid-containing extracts come from different plant materials and evidence chains, so the exact finished product still needs its own ingredients, responsible business, lot, label, authority, and report.
  • A field trial can describe one seed source, place, season, design, and measured outcome. It cannot guarantee another lot, latitude, planting date, management system, yield, chemistry, legality, market acceptance, or profitability.

Use the correct legal route for the place and date

Canada. Health Canada's 2026 list includes Finola and Finola2 and explains that licensed industrial-hemp cultivation must use pedigreed seed of an approved cultivar. Check the current list and licence conditions before relying on a dated entry. The list does not authorize every processed or finished use.

United States. USDA's Domestic Hemp Production Program routes production through approved state and Tribal plans or the USDA federal program where applicable. Producers must use the relevant licensing, reporting, sampling, testing, remediation, and disposal pathway. USDA's laboratory guidance bases compliance on a representative lot sample and total THC, accounting for potential conversion of THCA. A Canadian approved-cultivar entry or a seller's label cannot replace that process.

  • Record the jurisdiction and official program—not only the word “hemp.”
  • Verify licence or authorization before acquiring or planting where required.
  • Match the approved-cultivar rule, if any, to the exact year and seed evidence.
  • Preserve the production-lot identifier through sampling, results, harvest, storage, and disposition.
  • Check separate food, feed, extraction, transport, import/export, and finished-product rules.

Health Canada explicitly distinguishes hemp seed oil from CBD-containing products: cannabinoids are concentrated in other plant parts, while properly processed seed oil contains only trace carryover. Product names that blur those lanes deserve more documentation, not less.

Build the Finola verification card

  1. Start with the official identity.

    Save the CFIA record, exact denomination, earlier denomination, purpose description, authority, and access date.

  2. Check the current jurisdiction.

    Save the dated approved-cultivar list or production-program route and the licence, seed, sampling, testing, movement, and market rules that actually apply.

  3. Match the seed lot.

    Record supplier, producer, lot, label, class or certification, invoice, quantity, germination, purity, treatment, rights terms, and return terms. Do not fill missing fields with the cultivar record.

  4. Name the intended output.

    Oilseed, grain, research, fiber, flower, extract, food, feed, cosmetic, or another use creates different evidence, equipment, buyer, and legal questions.

  5. Preserve production and test identity.

    Carry the field and lot identifier into the authorized sample, laboratory report, harvest, storage, processing, sale, remediation, or disposal record.

  6. Verify the finished product separately.

    Match the responsible business, category, ingredients, package, lot, label, report, intended use, and current authority. A cultivar name cannot close these gaps.

  7. Keep a no-purchase or no-plant exit.

    If the identity, current rule, lot evidence, buyer, equipment, testing, or failure plan remains unresolved, delaying is a valid result.
