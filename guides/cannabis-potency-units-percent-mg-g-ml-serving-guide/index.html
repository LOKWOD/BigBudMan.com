Cannabis label math and comparison guide

Match the unit.
Then do the math.

Percent, milligrams per gram, milligrams per milliliter, milligrams per serving, and package totals answer different questions. This translator shows the valid arithmetic—and the comparisons that should stop.

Use the governing regulator's current definition, the exact product label, and the matched final report. Product rules and rounding conventions vary by jurisdiction and format.

THE USEFUL PART

Keep these in your head.

  • Before calculating, copy the analyte, result, unit, basis, product matrix, serving definition, package total, and source. A number without those fields is not comparison-ready.
  • For the same mass basis, 1% by mass equals 10 mg/g and mg/g divided by 10 equals percent. That exact unit conversion does not create a serving size, package total, delivered dose, or legal conclusion.
  • For a liquid, mg/mL multiplied by labeled container volume in mL gives labeled container milligrams. Do not convert mg/mL to mass percent unless the required density and governing definition are supplied.
  • Per-serving and package-total math is count arithmetic: mg per serving × number of servings. Verify that the printed serving size, count, and package total describe the same product and cannabinoid.
  • Total THC or total CBD formulas combine named acid and neutral analytes under a defined rule. Copy the regulator or laboratory formula and rounding rule; do not improvise one across jurisdictions.

Do not begin with the biggest number. Begin with whether the rows describe the same cannabinoid, basis, product, and evidence source. A 20% flower label, a 20 mg/mL liquid, and a 20 mg package total are not three versions of one value.

Convert percent and mg/g only on the same mass basis

Percent by mass → mg/g

mg/g = percent × 10

mg/g → percent by mass

percent = mg/g ÷ 10

The identity follows from the units: one gram contains 1,000 milligrams, and one percent is one part per hundred. Therefore 1% by mass is 10 mg per gram. A hypothetical 18% result on the same stated mass basis is 180 mg/g. This is concentration arithmetic—not a claim about what a person consumes, inhales, absorbs, or experiences.

Keep liquid concentration attached to volume

Container total from concentration

mg in container = mg/mL × labeled container mL

Concentration from a labeled total

mg/mL = labeled container mg ÷ labeled container mL

A hypothetical liquid labeled 20 mg/mL in a 30 mL container has a label-arithmetic total of 600 mg. That calculation checks internal consistency; it does not authenticate the product, validate the laboratory result, establish a serving, or recommend use.

!

Do not turn mg/mL into percent by guesswork

Milligrams per milliliter use mass divided by volume. Mass percent uses mass divided by mass. A valid conversion requires the product's density and the definition the responsible rule or method uses. Water-like assumptions are not evidence.

Reconcile serving, unit, and package totals

Package total

mg/serving × servings/package = mg/package

Per-unit amount

mg/package ÷ count = mg/unit only when each unit is one equal labeled unit

A hypothetical package listing 5 mg per serving and 10 servings has a calculated total of 50 mg for that same cannabinoid. If the package contains 20 pieces and defines two pieces as one serving, the per-piece arithmetic is 2.5 mg only if the label represents the pieces as equal and the governing labeling system permits that interpretation. Record both the serving size and the physical count.

Treat total-cannabinoid formulas as defined formulas

Regulators and laboratories may define total THC using delta-9 THC plus a molecular-weight adjustment for THCA. New York OCM's Part 128 guidance, for example, displays Total THC = THC + (THCA × 0.877) for specified labeling contexts. The factor does not mean that a consumer, kitchen, device, or body converts every milligram perfectly. It is a defined label calculation.

  • Copy every named input exactly: delta-9 THC, THCA, CBD, CBDA, or another analyte.
  • Confirm the inputs use compatible units and the same sample basis.
  • Copy the governing factor, formula, decimal handling, and rounding rule from the relevant authority or report.
  • Keep “total” separate from “active,” “potential,” “sum,” and other terms unless the source defines them as equivalent.
  • Do not apply one state's retail-label formula to settle federal hemp production, another state's market rule, or a clinical dose question.
