Cannabis appointment preparation

Bring the record.
Not the guess.

A clinician cannot reconcile “some gummies” with an incomplete medicine list. Turn the exact product, route, timing, reason, response, and open questions into one calm handoff.

General appointment-preparation education—not medical advice, diagnosis, treatment, a recommendation to use cannabis, or an emergency service. Do not start, stop, delay, or change cannabis, a prescription, or medical care based on this guide. Call 911 for an emergency. For a possible U.S. poisoning, call Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222.

THE USEFUL PART

Keep these in your head.

  • Bring the exact package or clear label photos. Record product name, route, labeled amount, serving basis, lot, ingredients, and when it was used.
  • List every prescription, over-the-counter medicine, vitamin, supplement, alcohol or nicotine exposure, and every route—not only pills.
  • Separate the goal from the result. Track symptoms and daily function before, during, and after use without diagnosing the cause yourself.
  • Ask who owns the next step, what to monitor, what would trigger urgent help, and when the plan will be reviewed.
  • A complete record does not prove safety, effectiveness, product purity, or legality. It gives the care team a better question to evaluate.

Start with one appointment question

Write the question at the top of the page before collecting details. Examples include: “Could this product contribute to dizziness?”, “Does this matter before my procedure?”, “Could CBD change how one of my medicines works?”, or “What should I monitor if I continue using this?” A question gives the visit a decision; it does not preselect the answer.

QUESTIONWhat decision do I need from this visit?
GOALWhich symptom, activity, or concern am I trying to address?
BOUNDARYWhat am I not asking the product to prove?
OWNERDoctor · pharmacist · surgeon · specialist · poison center · emergency team

If the question is about a medicine interaction, a pharmacist may be the most direct first stop. If it involves a procedure, pregnancy, severe symptoms, or a diagnosed condition, use the clinician or team responsible for that care. This page cannot decide which professional has enough context.

Bring the original package when safe and practical, or photograph every panel in good light. Do not move a product into an unlabeled bag for the visit. A strain name, package color, “full-spectrum” badge, or receipt line alone cannot reconstruct the exposure.

Build the whole medicine list—not just prescriptions

AHRQ medication-reconciliation materials ask people to include prescription medicines, nonprescription medicines, vitamins, herbal products, creams, ointments, patches, drops, sprays, inhalers, and injections. Add cannabis products, alcohol, nicotine, and other substances in a separate clearly labeled section so the reviewer can see timing and route.

  • Exact medicine or ingredient name, strength, form, amount, schedule, and reason it is used.
  • Prescriber and pharmacy where relevant, plus recent starts, stops, missed doses, or changes.
  • Known allergy or prior reaction and what actually happened.
  • Supplements, teas, sleep aids, pain products, cold remedies, and borrowed or leftover medicines.
  • Every cannabis and cannabinoid product, including CBD topicals, oils, drinks, capsules, flower, vapes, and products used only occasionally.

Do not stop a prescribed medicine or hide cannabis use to simplify the list. The purpose is to let a qualified professional evaluate the complete combination.

Use ordinary words. “Felt strange” is less useful than “stood up at 9:10 p.m., became light-headed, sat down, no fall, resolved in ten minutes.” A home measurement is only a recorded observation; it is not a diagnosis or a reason to delay urgent care.

Ask questions that produce an action

  1. Evidence.

    What is known for this exact goal, route, product type, and person—and what remains uncertain?

  2. Interaction.

    Which medicines or conditions create the most important concern? What sign would matter?

  3. Procedure or testing.

    Does this need to be disclosed to another team? Who gives stop, resume, fasting, driving, work, or testing instructions?

  4. Product.

    Which label fields or exact-lot records would change the assessment? Does a missing field make the product too uncertain?

  5. Monitoring.

    What should be recorded, how often, for how long, and what threshold changes the plan?

  6. Follow-up.

    Who owns the next step, by what date, and how should a new symptom or product change be reported?

Write the answers during the visit and read the plan back in your own words. If two professionals give different instructions, ask them to reconcile the difference rather than choosing the answer you prefer.

Use the urgent lane when the appointment can wait no longer

Call 911 for trouble breathing, loss of consciousness, seizure, chest pain, severe confusion, dangerous behavior, a serious injury, or another emergency. Do not drive an impaired or medically unstable person. For a possible U.S. poisoning or accidental exposure, call Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222; veterinary exposures need prompt veterinary guidance.

Preserve the package, remaining product, lot, receipt, and timeline after immediate care is addressed. Do not induce vomiting or use a social-media antidote. A scheduled appointment, web form, product return, or adverse-event report does not replace urgent care.

After the visit, update the medicine list and store the plan with the product record. If the product, medicine, symptom, procedure date, or use pattern changes, treat that as new information—not proof that the old plan still applies.
