Cannabis research literacy and evidence guide

Read the study.
Keep the boundary.

A journal headline can sound larger than the people, product, route, outcome, time window, and design it actually studied. Use this checklist to preserve the result and its limits in the same record.

Research-literacy education for adults 21+—not medical advice, diagnosis, treatment guidance, a systematic review, or a judgment that a cannabis product is safe, effective, legal, or appropriate for anyone. A checklist cannot repair a weak study or replace qualified clinical interpretation.

THE USEFUL PART

Keep these in your head.

  • Start with the exact research question and study design. A randomized trial, observational cohort, survey, case report, laboratory experiment, animal study, and review can answer different questions and carry different bias risks.
  • Keep the transfer screen visible: people, condition, cannabis product, cannabinoid formula, route, amount, schedule, comparator, outcome, and follow-up must match before a result can travel.
  • Statistical significance is not the same as practical or clinical importance. Record the absolute result, uncertainty interval, missing data, harms, and whether the outcome was selected before the analysis.
  • One study rarely settles a question. Look for replication, converging evidence, systematic reviews, current guidelines, and a qualified clinician who can apply evidence to the person and decision.
  • Do not turn an association into causation, an average into a guarantee, a short-term result into long-term safety, or a studied formulation into a retail product claim.

Write the study's question in one sentence

POPULATIONWho was included and excluded · condition · age · setting · prior cannabis exposure
EXPOSURE / INTERVENTIONExact cannabinoid or product · formula · route · amount · schedule · duration
COMPARATORPlacebo · usual care · another product · lower exposure · no comparator
OUTCOMEWhat was measured · by whom · with which tool · at what time
DESIGNRandomized trial · observational study · survey · case report · laboratory/animal study · review
QUESTIONIn [population], how was [intervention/exposure] related to [outcome] compared with [comparator] over [time]?

If that sentence cannot be completed from the title and abstract, open the full methods. If it still cannot be completed, mark the field unknown. Do not fill a missing product, amount, route, population, or outcome from a press release, retail page, or another paper.

Match the claim to the study design

DesignCan help answerCannot establish by itself
Randomized controlled trialWhether assigned interventions produced different measured outcomes under the trial conditionsUniversal benefit, long-term safety, every product, every person, or ordinary retail use
Observational cohort or case-control studyPatterns between real-world exposures and outcomes while measuring known covariatesCausation after confounding, selection, measurement error, and reverse causation remain possible
Cross-sectional surveyWhat a sampled group reported or measured at one pointWhich came first, causation, or change over time
Case report or case seriesA detailed signal worth investigatingFrequency, comparative risk, typical response, or cause
Laboratory, cell, or animal studyMechanisms and hypotheses under controlled nonhuman conditionsA safe or effective human dose, route, outcome, or retail-product conclusion
Systematic review or meta-analysisA structured synthesis of eligible studies and their consistencyQuality beyond the included evidence or a result that transfers outside the review question

ClinicalTrials.gov defines allocation, masking, observational studies, and outcome measures, but a registry entry is not peer review and registry staff do not verify scientific validity. Use the record to compare the planned study with the published report—not as proof that the conclusion is correct.

Run the ten-field transfer screen

FieldStudy recordDecision record
PeopleAge, diagnoses, exclusions, setting, baseline exposureDoes the person and situation actually match?
ProductNamed drug, standardized extract, flower, retail product, synthetic cannabinoid, isolated compoundIs the exact product the same object?
FormulaTHC, CBD and other measured ingredients; ratio and formulationDoes the package and lot report support a match?
RouteOral, inhaled, topical, or another routeIs the delivery path the same?
Amount + scheduleAssigned or observed exposure and adherenceIs comparison appropriate without inventing dose advice?
ComparatorPlacebo, standard care, other product, no useWhat does the result actually beat or differ from?
OutcomeScale, laboratory value, event, function, symptom, surrogateIs this the decision outcome that matters?
TimingFollow-up window and measurement pointsDoes a short window answer the long-term question?
ContextConcurrent care, medicines, other substances, environmentWhich co-exposures or supports differ?
RuleResearch authorization and jurisdictionDoes not establish product legality, approval, workplace policy, or clinical appropriateness

Read the result in both directions

  1. Find the prespecified primary outcome.

    Separate it from secondary, exploratory, subgroup, and post-hoc analyses. More comparisons create more opportunities for chance findings.

  2. Record the effect, not only the p-value.

    Capture the result in the original unit, the difference between groups, and the confidence or credible interval when provided.

  3. Ask whether the difference matters.

    A statistically detectable difference may be too small, uncertain, indirect, or short-lived to change care or function. A clinically important-looking difference may still be too imprecise to trust.

  4. Keep the denominator.

    Record how many people started, were analyzed, stopped, were lost to follow-up, or had missing outcome data.

  5. Read harms with benefits.

    Adverse events, serious adverse events, withdrawals, impairment, interactions, and monitoring belong in the same result card.

  6. Check the conclusion against the table.

    If the abstract says “effective,” “safe,” or “promising,” verify what the measured outcomes and uncertainty actually support.

Do the bias and transparency check

  • Was the protocol or trial registered before outcomes were known, and do the published outcomes match it?
  • How were participants recruited, assigned, masked, measured, retained, and analyzed?
  • Were groups different at baseline, and were important co-exposures measured?
  • Were the product identity, formula, route, adherence, and lot controls described well enough to reproduce?
  • Who funded the work, supplied the product, designed the study, analyzed the data, wrote the report, and holds relevant financial interests?
  • Are the protocol, statistical analysis plan, corrections, data-access statement, and supplementary results available?
  • Does the limitation section name the same weaknesses visible in the methods and results?

A disclosed conflict does not automatically invalidate a study, and an undisclosed conflict is not the only source of bias. Record the role and evaluate whether design, analysis, reporting, and independent replication reduce or amplify the concern.

Build a one-study claim card

CITATIONAuthors · title · journal · year · DOI/registry · correction/retraction check
QUESTION + DESIGNExact question · design · setting · recruitment dates
PEOPLEStarted · analyzed · exclusions · baseline · missing data
PRODUCT / EXPOSUREIdentity · formula · route · amount · schedule · duration · adherence
COMPARATORExact alternative and whether masking was plausible
PRIMARY RESULTOutcome · effect · absolute numbers · uncertainty · time point
HARMSEvents · serious events · withdrawals · monitoring · unknowns
TRANSFER LIMITSWhich people, products, routes, outcomes, and time frames do not match
BIAS + FUNDINGSelection · confounding · missingness · multiplicity · sponsor role · conflicts
NEXT EVIDENCEReplication · systematic review · guideline · clinician question

Safe conclusion template: “In [population], [intervention/exposure] was associated with or caused [measured result] versus [comparator] over [time], with [uncertainty/harms]. This does not establish [key transfer limits].” Use “caused” only when the design and execution support a causal comparison.
