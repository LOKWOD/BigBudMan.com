Keep the batch.

New York-focused household cultivation and recordkeeping education for adults 21+. It is not a harvest schedule, safe-moisture target, mold test, processing instruction, product-safety certificate, legal opinion, or permission to sell or transfer cannabis. Current law, the exact residence, building and fire rules, equipment instructions, and qualified local guidance control.

THE USEFUL PART

Keep these in your head.

  • Confirm the current home-cultivation rule, secure the post-harvest space, and record the household's plant and harvested-cannabis status before cutting anything.
  • Assign one batch identity at harvest and carry it through every rack, branch, tray, container, photograph, weight, condition note, and final storage location.
  • Log temperature, relative humidity, water or condensation, airflow state, equipment condition, odor, visible change, and access at consistent times; one display cannot certify the whole room or product.
  • Weight change is a trend, not proof that material is uniformly dry, free of mold or contaminants, ready for a sealed container, or safe to consume.
  • Stop on visible growth, musty or suddenly abnormal odor, wet pockets, condensation, pests, contamination, unexplained heat, damaged electrical equipment, or a broken identity chain. Do not trim, wash, heat, smoke, or extract a concern into safety.

Open with the law, residence, and possession record

New York OCM says adults 21 or older may cultivate at a private residence within person and household limits, must keep plants secure from people under 21, and must keep home cultivation out of public view. OCM also says home-cultivated cannabis is for personal use and cannot be sold. Those duties do not end when a plant is cut.

Before harvest, recheck the current OCM source, the exact residence, lease or property rules, local building and fire requirements, and the household's plant and harvested-cannabis record. Do not move material into a shared hallway, hotel, vehicle, workplace, self-storage unit, another residence, or outdoor common area to solve a space problem. The site's New York home-grow planner keeps plant count, lawful source, space, electrical load, and hard stops in the same file.

Give the harvest one durable identity

Create the batch ID before the first branch or portion leaves the plant record. The ID does not need to imitate a commercial tracking system; it needs to be unique inside the household and readable after racks, bags, trays, photographs, and later containers multiply.

Never recycle a prior ID, and never merge two plant or condition histories because the cultivar name looks the same. A branch found away from its label is unknown until the chain can be reconstructed; appearance and smell do not restore identity.

Choose a schedule that is realistic for the space and record extra checks after weather changes, outages, water events, equipment faults, room moves, or a condition concern. This guide intentionally does not publish a universal temperature, humidity, day count, stem test, or container-opening schedule. Plant structure, room, instrument, airflow, batch density, and intended lawful disposition differ, and a home target cannot certify a safe product.

Use mass change as a traceable trend—not a finish line

If a lawful household uses a scale, record the exact scale, unit, container or tare basis, batch portion, time, and handling state. Compare like with like. A whole hanging branch, trimmed flower in a tray, and material inside a container are not interchangeable measurements.

Δ

Keep the arithmetic narrow

Mass remaining (%) = current like-for-like mass ÷ starting like-for-like mass × 100. Mass change (%) = (starting mass − current mass) ÷ starting mass × 100. Example: the same labeled portion and tare moving from 400 g to 120 g has 30% of its starting mass remaining and a 70% mass change.

That calculation reports only the recorded mass trend. It does not reveal water activity, internal moisture distribution, mold, pesticide residue, pathogens, potency, decarboxylation, container readiness, smoke quality, or fitness for consumption. The scale repeatability guide can document a home instrument without calling it calibrated or legal-for-trade.

Make container entry a documented transition

Moving material from an open drying arrangement into a more closed container changes the moisture and inspection problem. Do not use a folklore day count, one snap test, the outside feel, a fragrance, or a humidity accessory as proof that every part is ready.

  1. Preserve the batch and portion IDs.

    Every container gets the source batch, subdivision, fill date/time, net material mass if measured, and responsible adult.

  2. Record why the transition was made.

    List the evidence used and every uncertainty. “Seven days” is a time record, not a safety conclusion.

  3. Use a compatible, clean, closable container.

    Record the material, prior use, clean/dry condition, usable volume, headspace, closure, label, and locked-storage fit.

  4. Continue condition checks.

    Record condensation, clumping, wet pockets, odor change, visible growth, temperature or humidity observations, and closure condition without treating a sensor as certification.

  5. Keep problems separate.

    Do not blend a questionable portion into a normal-looking container or move labels to make the record appear continuous.

A humidity pack is not a drying tool, mold remedy, or substitute for determining why material or a container is wet. The humidity-system guide separates instrument evidence, pack instructions, storage fit, and ownership cost.

Use the post-harvest stop gate

  • VISIBLE FUZZ, GROWTH, ROT, OR UNEXPLAINED DUSTStop handling that can spread material. Secure it, document the exact location, and seek qualified guidance. Do not scrape or trim the visible part into safety.
  • MUSTY OR SUDDENLY ABNORMAL ODOR, WET POCKET, OR CONDENSATIONSeparate the concern and investigate moisture and room conditions. Fragrance, ozone, smoke, heat, washing, freezing, or a fresh container does not establish safety.
  • PEST, CHEMICAL, CLEANER, PESTICIDE, SMOKE, OR FLOOD CONTACTPreserve the event, product, label, time, and affected portions. Do not consume, share, sell, or process uncertain material.
  • HEAT, SCORCHING, ARCING, WATER NEAR POWER, OR REPEATED TRIPStop using the affected equipment and space, keep people away, and use emergency or qualified electrical help as appropriate.
  • BROKEN IDENTITY OR UNEXPLAINED ACCESSMark the material unknown and keep it separate. A cultivar name, appearance, or household memory does not repair the evidence chain.

Home inspection cannot prove the absence of mold, microbes, mycotoxins, pesticide residue, heavy metals, or other contaminants. Do not smoke or ingest a sample to test whether a concern is real.

Keep the final record with the inventory and label kit. The record is useful even when the decision is to discard or seek help: it shows which room, container, tool, plant, and time require correction before another harvest.
