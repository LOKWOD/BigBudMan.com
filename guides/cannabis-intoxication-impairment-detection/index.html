Cannabis intoxication, impairment and detection terminology

Detected is not cleared.
Feeling fine is not proof.

Intoxication, impairment, detection, poisoning, and a legal violation are related questions—not synonyms. Each needs its own observation, test, rule, and decision-maker.

Call emergency services for collapse, seizure, severe trouble breathing, dangerous behavior, serious injury, or inability to wake.

THE USEFUL PART

Keep these in your head.

  • Intoxication describes drug effects; impairment asks whether function is reduced; detection asks whether a method found a named analyte under stated conditions.
  • A positive test does not automatically establish when use occurred, the degree of current impairment, or the applicable legal or workplace consequence.
  • A person who feels normal cannot use that feeling, a timer, a tolerance claim, or a home test as clearance to drive or do hazardous work.
  • Specimen, analyte, screening or confirmation status, cutoff, collection time, and program rule must stay attached to any result.
  • Emergency response starts with symptoms and safety—not debate about a number or product label.

Put each question in its own column

QuestionEvidence that may belongWhat it cannot decide alone
IntoxicationObserved or reported acute drug effects and clinical contextLegal guilt, exact dose, or a universal duration
ImpairmentTask-relevant changes in attention, coordination, judgment, reaction, perception, or behaviorCause, analyte concentration, or when every person is safe again

One event can occupy several rows, but the rows still should not be collapsed. A laboratory answers the question its method was designed to answer. A clinician evaluates health. An employer or agency applies its program. A court applies law. A website cannot replace any of them.

Function comes before the argument

Cannabis can affect reaction time, coordination, attention, judgment, perception, and decision-making. The effect depends on product, THC exposure, route, time, frequency, tolerance, other substances, sleep, health, and the task. Alcohol or other impairing drugs can increase risk and make attribution harder.

If a task can injure someone—driving, biking in traffic, boating, climbing, using power tools, cooking with open flame, supervising children, operating machinery, or making a safety-critical decision—remove the task before use. Do not wait for someone to “look high.” The driving guide explains why no countdown is a fitness test.

  • “I FEEL NORMAL”Self-assessment can miss changes in attention, judgment, coordination, or reaction.
  • “I USE EVERY DAY”Tolerance can change subjective effects; it does not provide task clearance or legal immunity.
  • “IT WAS ONLY CBD”The label may not establish product contents, THC exposure, interactions, or a testing exemption.
  • “THE NUMBER IS LOW”No universal blood-THC number can certify an individual's driving performance.

Ask whether a result is an initial screen, confirmed result, invalid specimen, dilute specimen, or another status. Record the analyte, cutoff, units, collection time, chain of custody, method, laboratory, and review process. The drug-testing guide covers the full screen-confirm-review sequence.

Why THC is not an alcohol-style meter

NHTSA's human-performance materials state that it is difficult to establish a relationship between a person's THC blood or plasma concentration and performance-impairing effects. THC concentrations change rapidly after inhalation, patterns differ with route and frequency, and metabolites answer different questions. A number can still matter under a particular statute or investigation, but science and law must not be substituted for each other.

Detection in a seriously injured or deceased road user also does not, by itself, prove that cannabis caused the crash. NHTSA prevalence reports make this limitation important: a detected drug category is a measured exposure in a defined sample, not a standalone causal verdict.

Keep three clocks separate

Time since use, time of observed function, and time of specimen collection may differ. Write all three instead of inventing one universal window.

Use the symptom gate first

  • CALL 911 NOWCollapse, seizure, severe trouble breathing, blue or gray color, severe chest pain, dangerous behavior, serious injury, or inability to wake.
  • POISON CONTROLIn the United States, call 1-800-222-1222 or use PoisonHelp.org for a suspected exposure when the person is stable enough to follow that route.
  • CHILD OR PETTreat any suspected exposure promptly. Keep the original package and estimated timeline; do not induce vomiting or wait for a test.
  • QUIET SUPPORTFor nonemergency anxiety or disorientation, reduce stimulation, remove driving and hazards, and have a sober adult stay nearby.

Use the overconsumption response guide for a fuller handoff record. A negative home test or low label amount does not overrule severe symptoms.

Preserve records privately. Do not post laboratory reports, specimen IDs, employment documents, medical histories, or legal notices publicly.

Science does not erase the governing rule

Jurisdictions use different approaches to drug-impaired driving, including observed impairment, toxicology evidence, per-se or zero-tolerance provisions, and combinations of evidence. Employers, federal programs, courts, athletic bodies, licensing boards, and medical settings can use different specimen types, cutoffs, review rights, and consequences.

Use the 50-state law library as a dated starting point only. Then read the current motor-vehicle code, program policy, laboratory notice, and appeal deadline. Do not use dilution, substitution, timing tricks, or adulterants; they can create health, employment, procedural, and legal risks.
