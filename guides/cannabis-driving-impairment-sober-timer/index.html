Responsible use and driving safety

No clock can
clear you to drive.

Cannabis can affect reaction, coordination, attention, judgment, and perception. A package, blood number, phone timer, or confident feeling cannot certify one driver's readiness.

11 min read Reviewed September 11, 2026Adults 21+

ON THIS PAGE

General public-safety education—not medical or legal advice and not a sobriety test. If you intend to drive, CDC says the safest option is not to use alcohol or drugs, including cannabis. Check current state law; when there is any doubt, do not drive or operate machinery.

THE USEFUL PART

Keep these in your head.

  • There is no universal wait time that proves an individual is ready to drive after cannabis; format, product, timing, co-use, medicines, sleep, and the person all matter.
  • CDC says it is difficult to connect the presence or concentration of THC to driving impairment in one person; NHTSA reports little evidence for a direct dose–blood-level–impairment relationship.
  • Feeling normal, driving slowly, passing a phone game, drinking coffee, sleeping briefly, or knowing a blood number does not establish driving fitness.
  • Choose a genuinely sober driver, rideshare, taxi, transit, or a place to stay before use begins, and protect the following morning when uncertainty remains.

Make one decision before the first use

The reliable decision is binary: either the transportation plan does not depend on the person using cannabis, or the plan is incomplete. Name a driver who will use no cannabis, alcohol, or impairing drug. Confirm the ride, transit hours, fare, pickup location, child-seat or accessibility needs, and overnight option while everyone can still solve logistics.

This applies beyond cars. Motorcycles, boats, ATVs, snowmobiles, bicycles in traffic, powered tools, ladders, forklifts, and safety-sensitive work also depend on attention, coordination, perception, and judgment. Legal possession does not authorize impaired operation.

!

The key does not stay in the experiment

Park legally, secure the vehicle, move keys out of the immediate decision, and keep the backup ride usable. Do not let the least-impaired person become the driver by default.

A conservative household rule can be stricter than law, but it still should not be marketed as a scientific guarantee. “Wait at least X hours” may be useful only when it comes from a clinician, employer, regulator, or product instruction for a defined context—and it does not override remaining impairment or local law.

What cannabis may change behind the wheel

CDC says cannabis can slow reaction time and decision-making, impair coordination, and distort perception. NHTSA summarizes experimental findings that include changes in lane position, following distance, reaction time, and divided attention, while also acknowledging inconsistent findings and study limitations.

That uncertainty is not evidence of safety. Driving asks several skills to work together under unpredictable conditions. A person may compensate by slowing down yet still miss a pedestrian, changing signal, merging vehicle, or second demand. Familiarity with the subjective feeling does not demonstrate normal performance.

  • Do not rehearse driving in a parking lot or on a quiet road.
  • Do not ask a passenger to “watch” an impaired driver.
  • Do not substitute cruise control, lane assistance, or self-described autopilot for a sober licensed driver.
  • Do not use a reaction-time app, balance test, eye check, or conversation as clearance.

A THC number is not an alcohol-style answer

CDC says it is difficult to connect the presence or concentration of THC to impairment in driving performance for an individual. NHTSA reports little evidence for a direct dose-response relationship among cannabis consumption, blood THC concentration, and driver impairment. Detection and impairment are related questions, not identical ones.

This does not make testing or impaired-driving laws irrelevant. Jurisdictions use different criminal, administrative, zero-tolerance, per-se, medical, and evidentiary rules. A person can face consequences under the applicable law even when a website cannot infer impairment from a number. Conversely, a low or unavailable number is not permission to drive when function is affected.

Use the site's 50-state law library only as a dated starting point, then confirm the current motor-vehicle code, regulator, and qualified legal advice where consequences matter.

Alcohol and medicines remove even more certainty

CDC says using multiple substances such as cannabis and alcohol at the same time can increase impairment. Sedating prescription medicine, sleep aids, antihistamines, opioids, benzodiazepines, and other products can add drowsiness, dizziness, slowed response, or interaction risk. Do not change a prescribed medicine merely to make room for cannabis or driving.

Tell a pharmacist or clinician the exact product, cannabinoids, format, amount, timing, medicines, supplements, alcohol use, and task. The cannabis-and-alcohol guide covers the combined-use plan; the medication guide organizes the professional conversation.

Seven things that do not prove sobriety

  • FEELING NORMALSelf-perception and every driving skill do not necessarily recover on the same schedule.
  • A HIGH TOLERANCEPrior experience does not certify reaction, divided attention, judgment, or legal fitness today.
  • COFFEE, FOOD, WATER, OR A SHOWERThese may change comfort; they do not instantly remove THC or establish performance.
  • DRIVING SLOWLYCompensation cannot remove surprise events or divided-attention demands.
  • A PHONE APP OR HOME TESTA game or screening result is not an individualized driving clearance.
  • A PACKAGE WAIT TIMEA label instruction may set a minimum caution; it does not override persistent effects or law.
  • SLEEPING FOR A WHILEIf grogginess, dizziness, slowed thinking, poor coordination, or uncertainty remains, do not drive.

If the original plan failed

  1. Do not start the vehicle.

    Stay where you are if safe, or move to a safe public or staffed place without entering traffic.

  2. Use the backup.

    Call the sober driver, taxi, rideshare, transit service, host, hotel, employer, or family member already identified.

  3. State the need plainly.

    “I am not safe to drive” is enough. Do not negotiate around embarrassment, cost, parking, or the morning schedule.

  4. Protect other people.

    Do not hand keys to another person who used cannabis, alcohol, or an impairing medicine.

  5. Handle the vehicle later.

    Retrieve it with a sober licensed driver and confirm parking or towing requirements.

If someone is trying to drive impaired and immediate danger exists, prioritize physical distance, sober help, venue staff, transit personnel, or emergency services as appropriate. Do not create a violent confrontation.

The next morning still requires a decision

A calendar date does not reset function. Before commuting, childcare transport, equipment use, or safety-sensitive work, notice residual sleepiness, dizziness, slowed thinking, poor coordination, nausea, confusion, or uncertainty. If any is present, postpone the task and use the backup plan.

Repeated near-misses, forgotten trips, pressure to drive, needing cannabis before driving, or difficulty keeping transportation rules are worth discussing with a qualified health professional. The tolerance and dependence guide separates adaptation, withdrawal, and patterns that need support.
