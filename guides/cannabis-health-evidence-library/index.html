THE USEFUL PART

Keep these in your head.

  • Start with the decision, not the desired conclusion. Emergency symptoms, a medicine interaction, a pregnancy question, a chronic-condition claim, and a product-label question need different sources and different urgency.
  • Keep four layers separate: what a study measured, what regulators have approved, what the exact product contains, and what is appropriate for one person. Evidence in one layer does not automatically answer the others.
  • Prefer current public-health, regulator, drug-label, and peer-reviewed sources. A citation can still be indirect, outdated, too small, based on a different route, or unrelated to the exact retail product.
  • Bring the exact product, route, label units, use pattern, medicines, symptoms, timeline, responsibilities, and goal to a clinician or pharmacist. Do not convert an unknown puff, drop, scoop, or piece into invented milligrams.
  • This page is a navigation asset. It does not rank conditions, promise benefit, replace emergency care, or make a retail cannabis or CBD product equivalent to an FDA-approved medicine.

Use an evidence ladder without pretending it is a scoreboard

  1. Urgent-care and safety guidance.

    Emergency instructions and poison-exposure routes answer what to do now. They do not prove a long-term diagnosis or explain every cause.

  2. Regulatory status and approved labeling.

    FDA approval addresses a specific drug, indication, formulation, evidence package, manufacturing standard, and label. It does not approve the entire cannabis plant, a cannabinoid category, or a similar-looking retail item.

  3. Clinical guidelines and systematic reviews.

    These synthesize evidence for defined patients and outcomes. Read the search date, inclusion rules, certainty, route, comparator, harms, and whether the question matches yours.

  4. Randomized or controlled human studies.

    Record population, intervention, dose, route, duration, comparator, outcome, adverse events, missing data, and funding. One trial rarely settles every decision.

  5. Observational human research.

    Useful associations can identify patterns and harms, but confounding, selection, measurement, reverse causation, and product variation can limit causal claims.

  6. Laboratory, animal, mechanism, or plant research.

    These studies can generate hypotheses. They do not establish a safe or effective human treatment, a personal dose, or the identity of a finished product.

  7. Testimonials and marketing.

    A story can describe one person's report. It cannot control bias, prove causation, disclose the complete denominator, or replace exact-product evidence.

The ladder is not a command to accept the highest-looking source. A current direct trial can still be small or irrelevant; a regulator page can be authoritative about approvals but not about one state's possession rule. Match the source to the decision it actually owns.

Audit a health claim in eight questions

  1. What exactly is claimed?

    Benefit, harm, diagnosis, prevention, treatment, cure, interaction, impairment, dose, or product quality?

  2. For whom?

    Adults, children, pregnant people, people with a condition, animals, cells, or plants?

  3. What exactly was studied?

    Whole plant, purified compound, prescription drug, extract, smoke, vapor, edible, topical, or a retail category?

  4. Against what?

    Placebo, standard care, another product, no exposure, or a laboratory control?

  5. Which outcome and time?

    Patient-important function, symptom score, laboratory marker, adverse event, short-term response, or long-term result?

  6. How certain and direct?

    Design, size, missing data, bias, confounding, replication, route, dose, population and product match?

  7. What changed since publication?

    Guideline, safety warning, approval, recall, product formulation, law, or later evidence?

  8. What action does the evidence justify?

    Emergency care, clinical discussion, pharmacist review, more verification, monitoring, rejection, or no conclusion?
